# Running — Pace Development Phase (post-ladder)

> This protocol begins **only after Stage 9 (30min continuous) is consolidated** — i.e. ≥2–3
> comfortable, symptom-free 30min runs. Until then, follow `skill/running.md` (the rehab ladder).
> The ladder builds tissue tolerance; this phase converts that base into speed. Read this file
> alongside `running.md` and `running-state.md` for any running question once the user is at Stage 9+.

## Ultimate goal

**Run 5K in under 35 minutes** (~7:00/km average) — injury-free, given a history of medial tibial
stress syndrome (MTSS), ankle ligament reconstruction, and knee problems.

Current baseline at entry to this phase: 30min continuous at ~10:00–10:30/km conversational pace.

## Governing principle (unchanged from rehab)

Tissue re-injury — not cardiovascular fitness — is the limiter. The evidence is unanimous: you get
faster by running **easier most of the time** and adding only a small dose of hard work. Never add
volume and intensity in the same week. Symptoms override all performance metrics, always.

## The model: 80/20 polarized

Evidence: Stöggl & Sperlich, *Frontiers in Physiology* 2015 (RCT — polarized beat threshold- and
volume-focused training: VO2peak +11.7%, time-to-exhaustion +17.4%).

- **~80% of running easy/conversational** (10:00–10:30/km at entry; drifts faster naturally).
- **~20% hard.**
- **Almost nothing in the "threshold" middle** — that's the trap zone (high fatigue cost, low reward).

Even as fitness improves, most runs stay genuinely easy. Speed comes from a small, deliberate dose,
not from grinding every run at a moderate-hard pace.

## Speed introduction ladder (safest order)

Introduce in this order, each only after the previous is tolerated symptom-free for 2–3 weeks:

1. **Strides first** — 4–6 × 15–20s relaxed accelerations at the end of an easy run, full recovery
   between. Near-zero injury cost; builds turnover and running economy. Always the first step.
2. **Short intervals** — e.g. 6 × 1min at ~8:00/km with easy jog recovery. Add only once strides
   feel fine.
3. **Tempo last** — sustained faster efforts load the tibia the most. Add only once intervals are
   well tolerated.

## Cadence — the single best MTSS lever

Evidence: Heiderscheit et al. 2011 — increasing step rate **5–10% above preferred** cut energy
absorbed at the knee by ~20% (at +5%) to ~34% (at +10%), reduced braking, lowered loading; prior
work showed reduced tibial acceleration.

Practical: measure current cadence, nudge it up ~5% with a metronome, keep pace the same. Shorter,
quicker steps = less shin load at any given speed. Revisit cadence whenever shins feel loaded.

## Strength & surface (protective factors)

- Keep the gym PPL work — it's protective against overuse injury.
- Add **direct calf raises + tibialis anterior (toe/tib) raises** — the lower-leg musculature
  buffers tibial load. This is the most targeted MTSS-prevention work.
- Prefer **softer surfaces (grass/trail)** when possible; keep shoes fresh. Hard surfaces and
  worn/tight calves are primary MTSS risk factors.

## Volume progression

- **10% rule** — don't grow weekly running time/distance more than ~10% per week. A conservative
  rule of thumb; for this runner, err *under* it.
- Never add volume and intensity in the same week.

## Realistic timeline (from Stage 9 consolidation)

| Window | Focus |
|---|---|
| Now → ~3 weeks | Reach & consolidate 30min continuous (Stage 9). |
| Weeks 3–8 | Add strides; nudge cadence up ~5%; hold 80/20. |
| Weeks 8–16 | Short intervals; easy-run pace drifts toward ~9:00–9:30/km at same effort. |
| Months 4–6 | Add light tempo; test a 5K — sub-35 should land here if shins stay quiet. |

## Symptom discipline (non-negotiable)

Any shin pain during or the morning after a run → drop the speed work, return to easy duration, do
not progress. This single discipline is what reaches sub-35 without a setback. Run the symptom check
from `running.md` after every session in this phase, not just the ladder.
